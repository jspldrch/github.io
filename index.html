Natural Disaster Severity Prediction

NYCU Data Mining, Spring 2026 · group Kaggle competition · metric: MAE

Each region in the dataset has 5,480 days of meteorological readings — wind, humidity, temperature at surface and dew point, precipitation — but a drought-severity score (0–5) recorded only once a week; the other six days are NaN. The task: take 91 days of history for a region and predict its severity for the next five consecutive weeks.

Approach

I treated it as a multi-horizon regression over engineered temporal features rather than a sequence model. Weekly targets were aligned to the last observed day of each 7-day block; features summarise both the 91-day window as a whole and its recent sub-windows, so the model sees the overall regime and the short-term trend into the forecast. Gradient-boosted trees, since the meteorological features sit on very different scales and the tree splits are scale-invariant.

What made it hard

The label sparsity — six of every seven rows unlabelled — and a private leaderboard drawn from a different, harder slice of the test set than the public one. The design choice that mattered was building the validation split to mirror that gap instead of chasing the public score.

Human Activity Recognition from Wrist Accelerometers

NYCU Data Mining, Spring 2026 · individual Kaggle competition · metric: macro-F1 · github.com/jspldrch/DataMining_Assignment3

100 users wore a wrist accelerometer; each 5-minute window carries one activity label (0–5) and 300 rows of per-second mean/std for three axes. The classes are severely imbalanced — two of them make up 85% of the data, one is 1.3% — and the test users are entirely unseen, so anything that memorises a person's individual movement signature fails on the leaderboard.

What worked

Dropping normalisation entirely was the single biggest gain (+0.017 F1). Per-user normalisation looked principled but erased the gravity projection in mean_z — which is class-specific signal, not user noise. Trees don't need scaled inputs, so keeping the raw values back paid off.

Temporal sub-windows — the same statistics recomputed over ten 30-second segments — added +6.7pp on cross-validation: how the signal evolves matters more than its global shape.

GroupKFold on user identity made local CV finally track the leaderboard, and a small heterogeneous ensemble (5×LightGBM + 2×XGBoost + 1×RandomForest) beat 30 copies of a single model — diversity over size.

What didn't

Deep learning (1D-CNN + LSTM) overfit the training users badly. Aggressive feature selection consistently hurt — the regularised trees handle 700+ correlated features better than any pruning I tried. Augmentation helped the small feature set but hurt the large one.
